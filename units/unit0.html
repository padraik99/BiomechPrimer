Unit 0: Planes, axes and joint actions

Order for this unit: 1 blind pretest, 2 primer, 3 explorer, 4 glass drill, then 5 retry when it opens in 2 days. Plan on about 2 sessions plus the retry.

Why guess before studying? Attempting questions before learning (and getting them wrong) improves later learning of that material compared with simply studying (the pretesting effect; Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009; Pan & Carpenter, 2023 review). You won't see answers now. Rate your confidence honestly: confident errors corrected later stick best (the hypercorrection effect; Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001).

Start here: anatomical position

Every direction, plane and action is described from anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, feet parallel, arms at the sides, palms facing forward. Think of it as the "zero" on a ruler. A joint action is a change away from this zero (or back toward it).

TermMeans
Anterior / posteriorfront / back
Superior / inferiortoward the head / toward the feet

The three cardinal planes

Rule 1: a plane is named for the slice it makes. Movement happens inside the slice, so the movement direction is not the direction in the name.

Sagittal

Slice: left from right, like a knife coming straight down your midline from the crown of your head.

Movement inside it: forward and back.

Feel it: stand side-on with your right shoulder touching a wall. Swing your right arm forward and back; your knuckles can brush the wall the whole way.

Frontal (coronal)

Slice: front from back. Think of separating the front cover of a book from the back cover.

Movement inside it: side to side.

Picture it: you are sandwiched between two panes of glass, one against your chest and one against your back. You can only move in ways that don't touch the glass: arms and legs out to the sides, bending sideways. Or picture a paper doll pinned flat to a wall: everything it can do without peeling off the wall is frontal.

Transverse (horizontal)

Slice: upper from lower, like a tabletop at your waist.

Movement inside it: rotation, like a turntable or revolving door.

Feel it: sit on a swivel chair, hold your arms out like a T and spin.

Drilling the frontal plane

The frontal plane is confusing for a predictable reason: the word "front" makes you think of forward motion. But forward motion is exactly what the frontal plane forbids. Two cross-wirings to memorise as a pair:

PlaneNamed for the slice between...Movement inside it goes...
Sagittalleft | rightforward / back
Frontalfront | backside to side
Transverseupper | loweraround (rotation)

A sentence to repeat: "The frontal plane separates front from back, so it is the side-to-side plane."

Wall test (do it now, 1 minute): stand with your heels, buttocks, shoulder blades and the back of your head against a wall. The wall is a frontal plane. Raise your arm out to the side: the back of your hand can slide along the wall (frontal). Raise it forward: it leaves the wall (sagittal). Bend sideways: you can slide (frontal). Bow forward: you leave the wall (sagittal). Turn to look over your shoulder: your shoulder leaves the wall by rotating (transverse).

Axes: the hinge pin

Rule 2: every movement rotates around an axis that is perpendicular to its plane. A door swings in a horizontal plane around a vertical hinge. Same idea for every joint.
PlaneAxis (perpendicular to it)Picture the skewer...
SagittalMediolateral (M-L)through both hip joints, side to side
FrontalAnteroposterior (A-P)through the joint from front to back, like a pin through the glass panes
TransverseLongitudinal (vertical)down the length of the limb or spine
Naming trap. Some textbooks call the M-L axis the "frontal" or "coronal axis" (because it lies in the frontal plane) and pair it with sagittal movement. So "frontal axis" means sagittal-plane movement. This course avoids that name and uses M-L, A-P and longitudinal: the axis is named for the direction it points.

Joint actions by plane (from anatomical position)

PlaneActions

TransverseMedial / lateral rotation (shoulder, hip); rotation (trunk, neck); pronation / supination (forearm); horizontal abduction / adduction (shoulder, hip from 90 degrees of flexion)

Flagged exceptions

1. Knee vs elbow. Both flexions are sagittal, but the forearm moves forward while the lower leg moves backward. The lower limb rotates medially during development, so its "front" ends up facing a different way. Flexion is defined at the joint, not by "forward".
2. Start position changes the plane. Horizontal abduction is still "abduction", but because the arm starts raised forward, it sweeps a horizontal slice. In Part 2 the same logic flips muscle actions: piriformis laterally rotates the hip in anatomical position but becomes a medial rotator when the hip is deeply flexed, because its line of pull crosses to the other side of the axis.
3. Thumb. The thumb sits rotated about 90 degrees from the fingers, so its flexion and extension are roughly frontal-plane and its abduction is roughly sagittal. We won't drill this; just know it is the odd one out.
4. Real movement is multiplanar. The cardinal planes are a reference grid, like latitude and longitude. Real joints such as the ankle and subtalar joint have oblique axes, and running uses all three planes at once. We name the main plane to reason about which muscles can produce or resist the movement.

Why this matters for you

  • Sprinting and the long jump: propulsion is mostly sagittal (hip, knee and ankle extension). The frontal plane is control: keeping the pelvis level on one leg. The transverse plane is the trunk and pelvis counter-rotating with the arm swing.
  • General running: excessive frontal-plane pelvic drop and knee collapse are common observation targets.
  • Everyday health: most training is sagittal (squats, lunges, walking). Frontal and transverse work, such as side steps and controlled rotation, covers the rest of how you move.

  • A&P 2e, 9.5 Types of Body Movements: the joint-action vocabulary with figures (about 20 min).

The dark dot is the nose, so you can always tell which way the figure faces. Things to try: (1) In Planes, slide the sagittal plane off-centre: it becomes parasagittal but is still sagittal. (2) In Movements, keep the frontal glass on and play every movement. Only frontal-plane movements avoid the crack. (3) Switch the glass to match each movement's plane: nothing ever cracks, because rotation around an axis never moves anything along that axis.

Predict, then watch. Glass panes sit against the chest and back (frontal planes). Before each movement plays, predict whether it will crack the glass. This is practice: you get instant feedback, and it doesn't touch your retry schedule.
